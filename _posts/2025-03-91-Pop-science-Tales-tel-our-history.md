We have all read or heard enchanting tales as children, stories that made us dream, tremble, laugh or think. But have you ever wondered where these stories come from?

To answer this question, we need to travel through the history of storytelling. Along the way, we will see that stories are not only deeply personal. They are also part of our shared human history.

We will first explore why humans tell stories and why storytelling appears in cultures all around the world. We will then see how stories change as they are transmitted from one generation to another.

Next, we will discover how methods developed in biology, particularly phylogenetics, can be used to reconstruct the history of stories in much the same way that scientists reconstruct the evolutionary relationships between species.

Finally, we will explore two particularly intriguing examples:

* [**Little Red Riding Hood**](#little-red-riding-hood)
* [**The Cosmic Hunt in the Berber Sky**](#the-cosmic-hunt-in-the-berber-sky)

So, where do stories come from, and how far into the past can we follow them?

## Why do humans tell stories?

Humans have been telling stories for tens of thousands of years, long before writing existed.

Like language, stories are a form of information transmitted from one generation to another. Through storytelling, we share experiences, convey ideas and pass on values that help shape the way we understand ourselves, our history and the world around us.

But a story is rarely transmitted without changing. As it passes from one person, generation or society to another, it can be modified, adapted or reinterpreted.

### Have stories evolved over the centuries?

Versions of the same story often reflect the moral values, concerns and social environment of the people who tell them. It is therefore not surprising that stories are repeatedly adapted to suit new audiences [1].

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG3_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>
</div>

Stories that are familiar to us today may be very different from earlier versions. Over time, storytellers introduce their own concerns, desires, fears and social realities into the narratives they inherit. Tales can therefore become snapshots of the societies that transmitted and transformed them.

A famous example is Jean de La Fontaine's fable *The Court of the Lion*. La Fontaine drew inspiration from an earlier fable attributed to Phaedrus, but reshaped the story to suit his own literary style and to reflect life at the court of Louis XIV.

Stories therefore preserve traces of the past, but they also constantly change.

### Is storytelling universal?

Stories can be found across centuries and continents, although individual tales are often closely associated with particular cultures and historical periods [2].

We might think of biblical stories such as Noah's Ark, Greek myths such as Narcissus, or much more recent legends such as Slenderman, which emerged on the internet in the early twenty-first century.

Other stories have become strongly associated with particular cultural traditions: *Peter and the Wolf* with Russia, *Hansel and Gretel* with Germany, *Le Petit Poucet* with France, and *Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves* with Persian and Arabic storytelling traditions.

Yet when we compare collections of traditional tales, something surprising appears.

If you leaf through the famous collection of the Brothers Grimm [3], for example, you begin to notice similarities between stories that occur in very different places. *Little Red Riding Hood* is a particularly striking example. Stories with similar characters and narrative structures can be found across several parts of the world.

How can we explain such similarities?

## Using biology to solve problems in folklore

One of the major questions in folkloristics, the study of traditional stories and customs, is deceptively simple:

**Where do stories come from?**

Answering this question is difficult because oral traditions leave few direct traces. Most tales were transmitted orally for centuries before anyone wrote them down.

In that sense, folklorists face a problem that resembles one encountered by palaeontologists. The evidence available today represents only a small fraction of what once existed.

To overcome this limitation, researchers have begun applying mathematical methods borrowed from evolutionary biology to the study of stories [4-6].

Just as biologists reconstruct relationships between organisms, folklorists can compare different versions of a story and attempt to reconstruct how these versions are related.

### But how does this work?

To understand the idea, it helps to begin with biological evolution.

The theory of evolution, developed during the nineteenth century by scientists including Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace [7], explains how populations change through time.

Organisms inherit characteristics from previous generations, but variation also occurs. Over many generations, these differences can accumulate and populations can diverge from one another.

One of the tools used to study these relationships is **phylogeny**, the reconstruction of evolutionary relationships among organisms.

The word comes from the Greek *phylon*, meaning tribe or race, and *genesis*, meaning origin or formation. In practice, phylogenetic methods allow scientists to reconstruct patterns of common ancestry.

### The link between phylogeny and fairy tales

What does this have to do with stories?

The anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss observed that myths and stories can change through processes that resemble biological evolution [8].

A story is inherited from previous storytellers, but it is rarely copied perfectly. Elements are removed, added or transformed. As the story spreads between communities, different versions gradually emerge.

Researchers can therefore compare the characteristics of different versions of the same tale.

These characteristics are sometimes called **mythemes**, basic narrative elements that can be recorded and compared across stories.

They might include the gender of the protagonist, the identity of the antagonist, the location of the story, the way a character escapes, or the ending of the tale.

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG5_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>

Researchers can treat these narrative characteristics somewhat like biological traits.

If two versions of a story share many unusual features, they may have inherited them from a relatively recent common ancestor. By comparing many versions, researchers can construct a tree representing possible relationships between them.

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG6_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>
</div>

This does not mean that stories evolve exactly like organisms. Storytellers can borrow elements from several traditions at once, and similar ideas can sometimes appear independently.

Nevertheless, phylogenetic methods provide a powerful additional tool for exploring the history of oral traditions.

## Little Red Riding Hood

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG9_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>
</div>

*Little Red Riding Hood* is one of the best-known fairy tales in the world.

At its centre is a simple but powerful confrontation between vulnerability and danger. Yet the story that most of us know today represents only one branch of a much larger family of tales.

### The classic tale

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG7_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>
</div>

The version familiar to many European readers derives largely from the tale written by Charles Perrault in the late seventeenth century [9,10], followed by the version later popularised by the Brothers Grimm [3].

But not everyone knows the same story.

In some versions, there is no heroic hunter. Instead, the girl escapes from the predator herself by pretending that she needs to go outside. In others, the famous red hood does not appear at all.

The characters, setting and ending can vary considerably.

What remains recognisable is the underlying structure of the story.

### Strangely similar tales

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG8_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>
</div>

Stories with striking similarities to *Little Red Riding Hood* can be found in several parts of the world, including Africa and East Asia [10,11].

This raises an interesting question.

Do all these stories descend from a common ancestral tale, or did similar narratives emerge independently?

Phylogenetic methods provide one way of investigating this question.

### Little Red Riding Hood: an old story

Long before phylogenetic approaches were applied to folklore, scholars had noticed similarities between *Little Red Riding Hood* and another European tale, *The Wolf and the Seven Young Goats*.

The two stories share several narrative elements. In both, a dangerous animal deceives vulnerable characters and gains access to them.

For a long time, researchers debated whether the stories represented variants of a single ancestral tradition or two separate tales.

Jamshid Tehrani addressed this question using phylogenetic methods. He compared 58 versions of the stories and coded 72 different plot characteristics [12].

Constructing such a dataset requires reading, comparing and classifying a large number of narratives. Inevitably, some decisions about how individual story elements should be defined can be debated [13].

Nevertheless, this approach made it possible to test hypotheses about the relationships between the different traditions in a systematic way.

### Geographical origins

The analysis suggested that *Little Red Riding Hood* and *The Wolf and the Seven Young Goats* represent distinct European story traditions rather than versions of a single recent tale.

Possible ancestors of the *Little Red Riding Hood* tradition can be traced back to the Middle Ages [14].

African variants appear more closely related to *The Wolf and the Seven Young Goats*. One hypothesis is that versions of this tradition reached parts of Africa through historical contacts and were subsequently transformed into local forms that spread through central and southern Africa and eventually reached the Caribbean.

The East Asian tale *The Tiger and the Grandmother* appears to contain features associated with both European traditions. It may therefore represent a more complex history involving the combination of elements from different stories.

Rather than revealing one simple point of origin, the analysis illustrates how stories can branch, travel and sometimes recombine as they move between populations.

This example concerns a story that may be several centuries old.

But can similar methods take us further into the past?

## The Cosmic Hunt in the Berber Sky

### 15,000 years ago...

Let us now turn to a much older possible storytelling tradition.

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG10_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>
</div>

During the Palaeolithic, humans lived in environments very different from our own.

Many of the objects, social institutions and technologies that structure modern life did not exist. Hunter-gatherers interacted directly with landscapes, animals and seasonal cycles that shaped much of their daily existence.

Their stories have disappeared almost completely from the historical record.

Yet some researchers have asked whether fragments of those very old traditions might survive in myths told thousands of years later.

### The myth

One particularly interesting example is a family of stories known as the **Cosmic Hunt** [15].

Versions of this myth have been recorded in Africa, Europe, Asia and the Americas.

The details vary, but many versions share a central narrative: one or more hunters pursue an animal, and the characters involved in the hunt eventually become stars or constellations.

<div class="row mt-3">
    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">
        {% include figure.liquid path="assets/img/FIG11_tales.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true %}
    </div>
</div>

Among some Iroquois peoples of North America, for example, three hunters pursue a bear. Its blood colours the autumn leaves as it flees, before the bear and hunters eventually become associated with stars in the sky.

Among the Chukchi of Siberia, Orion can represent a hunter pursuing a reindeer, while other constellations also play roles in the celestial narrative.

How can such broadly similar stories appear among populations separated by enormous geographical distances?

One possibility is **convergence**. Different societies may have independently created similar stories because humans everywhere observe animals, hunting and the night sky.

Certain themes could therefore emerge repeatedly without requiring a common origin.

Another possibility, however, is that some of these stories are historically related.

    <div class="col-sm mt-3 mt-md-0">

## The phylogenetic study

Julien d'Huy explored this possibility by comparing 47 versions of the Cosmic Hunt and coding 93 narrative characteristics [15].

The geographical distribution of the myth is particularly interesting.

Versions are uncommon in Australia and are almost absent from Indonesia and New Guinea. In contrast, related traditions occur on both sides of the Bering Strait.

This matters because during the last Ice Age, sea levels were much lower than today. The region that is now submerged beneath the Bering Strait formed part of Beringia, a broad land connection between Siberia and Alaska [16,17].

Human populations moving from Eurasia into the Americas crossed this region.

The combination of geographical patterns and phylogenetic reconstruction led d'Huy to suggest that at least part of the Cosmic Hunt tradition could be extremely ancient, potentially originating among prehistoric Eurasian populations before some of their descendants entered the Americas.

Related versions recorded in North Africa have also been interpreted in the context of ancient population movements between Eurasia and Africa.

If this hypothesis is correct, elements of the story may have travelled with human populations over thousands of kilometres and survived through countless generations of oral transmission.

The study even attempted to reconstruct some characteristics of an ancestral form of the myth.

In this proposed reconstruction, a large herbivore is pursued by a hunter. During the chase, the animal reaches the sky and becomes associated with a group of stars, perhaps the constellation we now identify as the Big Dipper.

Such a reconstruction can never be treated as a literal transcript of a story told 15,000 years ago. Too much has changed, and too much information has been lost.

What the analysis provides instead is a hypothesis about the kinds of narrative elements that may have existed in very old storytelling traditions.

Other studies have reached similarly striking conclusions. The Indo-European tale known as *The Smith and the Devil*, for example, has been investigated using comparative phylogenetic methods and proposed to have roots extending back to the Bronze Age [18].

## Conclusion

Stories are remarkably mobile.

They travel between people, generations and cultures. They change as they move, but they can also preserve surprisingly old elements.

For most of human history, these traditions were transmitted without books, recordings or written archives. A story survived only because someone remembered it and chose to tell it again.

This makes the possibility of reconstructing their history particularly fascinating.

Phylogenetic approaches do not allow us to recover ancient stories word for word, nor can they provide definitive answers about every tale. Cultural evolution is complex. Stories can merge, borrow elements from one another, disappear and sometimes independently develop similar themes.

But these methods give folklorists another way to investigate questions that were once extremely difficult to approach.

They also remind us that stories are more than entertainment. They are part of a cultural inheritance that connects people across generations and, in some cases, perhaps across thousands of years.

Our ancestors once told stories around fires beneath the night sky. Today, stories can travel around the planet in seconds.

The ways we tell them have changed enormously.

Our desire to tell them has not.
